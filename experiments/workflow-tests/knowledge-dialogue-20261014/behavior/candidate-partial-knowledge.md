I’d explore three different reasons someone might feel comfortable returning. These are hypotheses, not findings about the workshop’s customers.

1. **Make their progress visible.** Use the follow-up message to connect what they did in class to one achievable next step: “You tried shaping a bowl; next time, you could practise getting an even wall.” Where possible, refer to their actual piece or technique. This gives returning a purpose beyond “take another class.” The tradeoff is a little instructor attention, and the invitation should feel optional rather than like homework.

2. **Make returning feel socially familiar.** Offer a clear opportunity to return with the same instructor or alongside other beginners, if the schedule supports it. The invitation could explicitly welcome people coming alone. This direction addresses belonging and familiarity; its constraint is scheduling and the effort needed to sustain that welcome.

3. **Remove uncertainty about the second visit.** Make the existing booking page answer practical questions: Which class suits someone who has attended once? What will they do? What does it cost, and what should they bring? Link directly to that option from the follow-up. This is relatively simple, but clearer booking only helps if uncertainty is what holds people back.

**I recommend starting with visible progress.** It connects the second visit to something the person has already experienced and fits the workshop’s existing follow-up message without adding a new service.

The useful distinction from the supplied first-value brief is that completing an activity and experiencing something worthwhile are different. The message should help someone recognise a real accomplishment and see a possible continuation—not assume that attending once proves they want more. If people already express enthusiasm but struggle to choose their next class, I’d prioritise the third direction instead.
